\section{C3: What moved the score, priced in steps}
\label{sec:findings}

More optimizer steps in the same 30 minutes and a handful of structural changes drove most of the descent in
\cref{fig:history}. This section prices the changes with $\kappa$ (\cref{sec:price}) and gives each one a noise scale;
the evidence for each finding, and the campaign's step-count gains broken down by kind (\cref{sec:steps}), are in
\cref{app:findings}.

\subsection{Levers, their noise and their price}
\label{sec:levers}

\Cref{tab:levers} lists the changes that helped. The noise column is the SD of a single measured difference under the
order-redraw noise model of \cref{sec:seeds}: $\sqrt2$ times the shuffle-salt SD for a chip pair
(\val{noise-chip-pair}), plus the offset noise of two uploads for an official pair (\val{noise-official-pair}),
divided by $\sqrt n$ for $n$ pairs. It is the right scale for changes that act after the warm-up; a change that
re-rolls the warm-up path faces the larger seed noise. Four of the six largest effects (multi-token prediction,
batch warm-up, LeakyReLU$^2$, the rotary key offset) come from the campaign log and have no run identifiers in the
repository, so the table gives them no ratio to a noise scale; the late levers are single pairs at seed~73. Of the
levers with a recorded comparison, only the row pool and attention-source reuse clear about three noise SDs. The
table lists survivors, so its effects are biased away from zero, and for levers that act from step~0 the ratios are
upper bounds (\cref{sec:lever-notes}).

\subsection{The findings in brief}

The row pool, a row-level shuffle buffer on the streaming loader, gave \val{rowpool-chip}\docnum{} in one chip pair at
one seed, about \val{rowpool-z} order-noise SDs; its official delta of \val{rowpool-official} replays the same
trajectory, so it confirms the oracle, not the effect (\cref{sec:rowpool}). Attention-source reuse is our
best-replicated structural effect, with a mean of \val{asr-mean} over four same-seed chip pairs (\cref{sec:asr}). The
EMA update, compiled inside the charged clock on a fresh instance, cost \val{ema-compile-steps}\docnum{} steps that
only cold rehearsals revealed; the fix runs one EMA update during the excluded start-up (\cref{sec:prewarm}).
Seeds take one of two warm-up paths, and spike-path runs ended \val{spike-delta} worse on average; seed and
shuffle-salt noise are as large as most late improvements, and a salt chosen by a lottery keeps in expectation a
share $\lambda$ of its rehearsal edge, \val{wc-lam-lo}--\val{wc-lam-hi} when every draw ran on one chip and
\val{wc-lam-cross-lo}--\val{wc-lam-cross-hi} when they ran on different chips (\cref{sec:seeds,sec:salts}).

Capacity changes lost in both directions, and scalar retuning found nothing: none of \val{scalar-keys}\docnum{}
scalar knob keys won a confirmation, with little power per key (\cref{sec:negative}). Finally, \cref{eq:exchange}
converts our gap to the top ten into about \val{gap-ten-range} more
effective compute, which we offer as a hypothesis that kernel throughput was the cheapest remaining lever, not as a
finding (\cref{sec:gap}).
