\section{Introduction}

Training speed-runs fix a budget and ask how low a validation loss can go. Community records such as the
modded-nanoGPT speed-run \citep{jordan2024moddednanogpt}, built on nanoGPT \citep{karpathy2022nanogpt}, academic
``cramming'' studies \citep{geiping2023cramming,izsak2021academic}, budgeted-training analyses \citep{li2020budgeted}
and time-to-result benchmarks such as DAWNBench \citep{coleman2017dawnbench}, MLPerf Training
\citep{mattson2020mlperf} and AlgoPerf
\citep{dahl2023algoperf} share one property: the quantity being optimised is the quality that a \emph{wall-clock}
budget buys, not the quality per step or per FLOP. In such a setting two things are scarce. A full trial costs the
whole budget on the target hardware, and the official score comes from the organisers with limited feedback. Most
practical decisions therefore rest on proxies, such as short screens, scores on public data and runs on other
hardware, whose relation to the official score is often left unmeasured.

This paper reports how one team measured that relation in Phase~1 of the AWS Trainium Frontier challenge
\citep{trainiumfrontier2026rules}. A language model is trained from scratch for 30~minutes of charged time on one
\texttt{trn2.3xlarge} instance and scored by validation bits per byte (\valbpb{}) on a pinned validation shard
computed by the organisers. Over 17~days and \val{n-scored} scored uploads, our official score fell from
\val{first-official} to \val{best-official} (\cref{fig:history}). Our best upload finished outside the top ten, so
this is not a paper about a winning recipe. It is a measurement study: \val{n-rehearsed} of the \val{n-scored}
uploads were preceded by a full-length rehearsal on our own hardware (the other \val{n-official-tests}, \upl{K65a}--\upl{K65d}, were
official tests of single changes), failures were logged as carefully as successes, and the repository recomputes
every number not marked \docnum{} from the released data.

\paragraph{What is and is not new.} Replaying a candidate on hardware that behaves deterministically and correcting it
with a hold-out offset is standard validation practice, and a ridge regression with era, chip and seed effects is a
standard surrogate. How well proxy evaluations transfer to a target has been studied for test sets, benchmarks and
small-scale pretraining experiments, and benchmark organisers have analysed time-to-result competitions
(\cref{sec:related}). Our contribution is a participant-side \emph{measurement} in one wall-clock contest: how well
that practice predicted a real leaderboard, how large its errors and biases were, what limited it, and what a single
price per optimizer step says about the changes we tried. We have not found these quantities reported for a
time-budgeted training contest, and we think they are useful to anyone running or entering one.

\paragraph{Contributions.} In one sentence: in a wall-clock contest, a cold, full-length rehearsal of the exact
upload predicted the official score up to a measurable offset, while a cheaper simulator could tell which changes
were inside the noise but not which would win. The four contributions below, in this order, structure the paper.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1: How well full-length rehearsal predicts a leaderboard} (\cref{sec:oracle}). A cold,
    full-length rehearsal of the exact upload file, scored on the first \val{eval-tokens} public validation tokens,
    predicted the official score up to an offset of \val{off-mean-since} (SD \val{off-sd-since}, $n=\val{off-n-since}$).
    The offset is mostly a property of the text (the official shard is harder than the first 2M public tokens) but appears to carry
    a per-instance component of a few $10^{-4}$, confounded with salt selection. A calibration frozen on the chip-C
    calibration uploads predicted the seven final uploads with MAE \val{holdout-mae} and a positive bias on faster
    instances; we report how much of this depends on one normalised rehearsal, on salt selection and on the burn-in of
    the interval.
  \item \textbf{C2: A run simulator, with baselines} (\cref{sec:sim}). \ffsim{} decomposes a score into steps,
    computed by replaying the training script's charged-time schedule, and quality per step, from a ridge surrogate
    fitted on \val{q-nfit} fully specified chip runs out of \val{ds-n} harvested records. Leaving one pair out
    (replicate prediction), it beats a steps-only baseline on recipe changes (paired difference in sign agreement
    \val{base-diff-sign-ci}) and scores above a majority sign (\val{base-major-rec-sign}); forward in time its knob
    features add little; on new mechanisms it is no better than chance. A GPU proxy that replays the chip's schedule
    is a feasibility demonstration (\cref{sec:gpu}).
  \item \textbf{C3: An accounting of what moved the score} (\cref{sec:price,sec:findings}; evidence in
    \cref{app:findings}). One price,
    $\kappa=\val{kappa}$ bpb per unit $\ln(\text{steps})$, converts every change into optimizer steps. With it we
    report the levers with their noise, a row-level shuffle pool (\val{rowpool-chip}\docnum{} in one chip pair at
    one seed; the official \val{rowpool-official} replays the same trajectory, so it confirms the oracle, not the
    effect), a compilation-accounting trap that only cold rehearsals reveal, the size of seed and order noise,
    negative results, and a pricing of the gap to the top ten (\val{gap-ten-range} more effective compute) that we
    offer as a hypothesis about kernels, not a finding.
  \item \textbf{C4: Released artifacts} (\cref{sec:contrib}). The simulator, the run dataset, both recipes, a
    local app and a reviewed, guarded pipeline through which others can add runs to the simulator. No external
    contributions exist yet.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Scope.} This is one team's campaign on one contest and one hardware type, and most late effects rest on
one or a few same-seed pairs at a seed that was itself selected. We give $n$ and a noise scale for every effect, mark
numbers that exist only in campaign documents with a dagger (\docnum{}, listed in \cref{app:docfacts}), and list the
threats to validity in \cref{sec:limits}. Other teams appear only as rank-indexed scores from the public leaderboard
snapshot.

\paragraph{Roadmap.} After related work (\cref{sec:related}), \cref{sec:setting} describes the contest, the

present C1--C4 and \cref{sec:transfer,sec:limits} what transfers and the limitations. The appendices hold the
evidence behind C3 (\cref{app:findings}), the upload ledger (\cref{app:ledger}), oracle and simulator details
(\cref{app:oracle,app:sim}), the contribution pipeline (\cref{app:contrib}), derivations and notation.
